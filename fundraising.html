Field Trip Fundraising Toolkit

Plan and pay for college tours and other field trips. Estimate what the trip costs, pick fundraisers that fit your time, check your numbers, and use the ready-to-edit letters and posts. Written for California public high schools in Tulare County and the Central Valley.

Every student goes, paid or not. In California, a student can't be left off an educational field trip because the family can't pay. Treat fundraising and family donations as voluntary, and don't tie a student's spot on the trip to how much they raised. See Rules and approvals.

5Rules and approvals in California

This is general guidance, not legal advice. Laws and district policies change, and every district does things a little differently. Before you start, confirm your plan with your ASB advisor, your principal, and your district business office. When this page and your district disagree, follow your district.

✅ Get approval before you start

  • Fundraisers usually need advance approval. Depending on your site, that can mean an ASB fundraiser request (often approved by the student council or club, the ASB advisor, and the principal), a principal sign-off, and sometimes district approval.
  • Ask early which account the money belongs in: an ASB club account (for example, an AVID club) or a district or site account. The rules for spending each are different, and ASB money is meant for student activities chosen by students.
  • The field trip itself has its own approval: your district's field trip request, and often board approval for overnight or out-of-state trips. Plan for both.
  • Chaperones may need district volunteer clearance (such as fingerprinting or a TB screening). This can take weeks, so start early.

Helpful reference: the FCMAT "ASB Accounting Manual, Fraud Prevention Guide and Desk Reference" (fcmat.org), which many California districts follow.

💵 Handle money the safe way

  • Receipts: give a receipt for money you collect, using the receipt book or system your ASB bookkeeper provides. Keep a written log of what came in.
  • Two-person counts: count cash with a second adult, fill in a count sheet, and have both people sign it.
  • Deposit through the ASB bookkeeper (or the district's process) the same day or as soon as possible. Don't leave cash in a classroom overnight.
  • Never put fundraiser money in a personal bank account, and don't collect it through personal apps like a personal Venmo, Cash App, or PayPal. Use only the district-approved online payment system, if there is one.
  • Pay trip costs (buses, admission) through purchase orders or the business office, not out of pocket and not from cash on hand.
  • If you sell products (shirts, plants, coupon books), ask the bookkeeper whether sales tax applies.

🥨 Food sold to students on campus

  • Food and drinks sold to students on campus during the school day must meet the federal Smart Snacks in School standards and California's nutrition standards. California's rules are stricter in some areas.
  • Under the federal rule, the "school day" runs from midnight before until 30 minutes after the official end of the school day. Check your district's timing rules before selling anything that isn't compliant, even after school.
  • California has extra rules for food sales by student organizations (in the California Code of Regulations, Title 5), such as limits on what can be sold and when. Your district's food service director can explain them.
  • Food sold at evening games and events may still need a county health permit if it's prepared or handled. Check with your district and the Tulare County Environmental Health office.

Learn more: USDA "Smart Snacks in School" and the California Department of Education (cde.ca.gov) pages on competitive foods.

🎟️ Raffles are strictly regulated

  • In California, raffles are allowed only under Penal Code 320.5, and only for eligible nonprofit organizations that register with the California Attorney General every year and follow strict rules (for example, at least 90 percent of gross receipts must go to charitable purposes).
  • Many districts don't allow class or club raffles at all. Don't run a raffle, "opportunity drawing," or any game where people pay for a chance to win a prize without written approval from your district.
  • Safer swaps: sell votes in a "pie a teacher" contest (the most money wins, not a random draw), or ask local businesses to donate items you give as thank-you gifts to every sponsor.

🤝 Donations from businesses

  • Donations of money or goods to a school may need a district donation form, and larger gifts may need school board acceptance. Ask your office before you promise a business anything.
  • Checks should be made payable to the school or district (ask which name to use), never to you.
  • Don't give tax advice. If a donor asks about a receipt for taxes, refer them to your district business office and use the district's official receipt if one exists.
  • Be careful about what you offer sponsors in return (logos on shirts, signs). Your district may have advertising and logo rules.

⚖️ No student is left out for lack of money

  • California's Constitution guarantees free public education, and Education Code sections 49010 to 49013 prohibit charging students fees to take part in educational activities.
  • Education Code 35330 lets districts pay for field trips and allows them to ask for money, but says no student may be prevented from going on a field trip because there aren't enough funds.
  • So: ask families for voluntary donations, never required payments. Don't set a required amount each student must raise, and don't make a student's spot depend on fundraising. California Department of Education guidance on student fees treats required fundraising as a fee.
  • Plan the budget so the trip happens for everyone. Keep who paid or raised what private; don't post individual totals.

Check the current text at leginfo.legislature.ca.gov and CDE's "Pupil Fees" guidance (cde.ca.gov).
